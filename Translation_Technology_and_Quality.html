Translation Technology, Meaning, and Quality

Scope and main conclusion

Translation quality is not a single property. A useful translation must preserve the source’s intended meaning, express it naturally in the target language, and fit its audience and use. These aims can pull in different directions, so a fluent sentence is not necessarily faithful, and a close rendering is not necessarily the best translation. Research on machine translation (MT) increasingly supports evaluating these dimensions separately rather than treating one score as a complete verdict [1], [2].

This report draws on the workspace search Translation Technology and Meaning, completed November 15, 2026. Its evidence ranges from foundational translation theory and MT evaluation to recent studies of large language models (LLMs), idioms, and human–AI workflows. The strongest practical conclusion is to treat automated scores as screening and comparison tools, then judge meaning, idiomaticity, and fitness for purpose with methods suited to the text and users.

Translation technologies and services

Translation technology has changed from hand-built linguistic rules to systems that learn from translated text, and now to general-purpose language models. Each shift expands what can be automated, but none removes the need to check meaning, context, and target-language use. This is a history of changing tradeoffs, not a simple march toward error-free translation [3].

Human translation with computer-assisted tools

What it contributesTranslation memories, terminology resources, and other computer-assisted translation tools can support consistency and reduce repetitive work while leaving choices with the translator [4].

Main limits or service implicationsTools do not determine whether a stored phrase fits the present context, audience, or intent.

Rule-based MT

What it contributesUses dictionaries and explicit linguistic rules; transfer and interlingua designs represent grammatical structure or meaning between languages [3].

Main limits or service implicationsRules and linguistic resources take substantial effort to build and maintain, and can be difficult to scale across languages and domains.

Statistical MT

What it contributesLearns likely word and phrase correspondences from parallel texts; phrase-based systems can model some local reordering [3].

Main limits or service implicationsLimited context and difficulty with long-range structure, rare expressions, and semantic nuance.

Neural MT

What it contributesLearns end-to-end mappings; attention and Transformers improve contextual processing, while multilingual and pretrained models extend coverage [3].

Main limits or service implicationsData imbalance, domain shift, and difficult language directions persist. Fluency does not guarantee source fidelity.

LLM-based translation

What it contributesPrompted models can translate with little task-specific training and can follow instructions about tone, style, or audience [3].

Main limits or service implicationsPrompt sensitivity, omissions, additions, hallucinations, and uneven language performance remain concerns; specialized MT may still offer efficiency, terminology control, or consistency.

Human–AI workflows

What it contributesTranslators may use MT or LLM suggestions, then review, revise, or reject them; post-editing can save time in some settings [5], [6].

Main limits or service implicationsProductivity and quality gains depend on the system, text, language pair, and editor. A poor draft can take longer to repair than to translate from scratch.

In professional services, the relevant choice is often not “human or machine” but which tasks to automate, what review to require, and who is accountable for the final text. A review of post-editing research found that gains vary: some studies report faster work, while others find little or no time benefit. Error type matters too; mistranslated idioms and word-order problems can demand more effort than simple form edits [5]. In one small English-to-Chinese student study, post-editing lowered measured pausing and improved some assessments of raw MT, but raters still preferred from-scratch translations more often. The authors caution that the sample was limited and the participants were not experienced professional translators [7]. These findings argue against treating an average productivity figure as a guarantee for a particular service or project.

Conveying meaning without calques

Avoiding calques does not mean avoiding literal translation whenever possible. The problem is a rendering that follows source-language wording or structure so closely that it sounds unnatural, misstates the intended sense, or fails in its target context. The relevant test is whether the translation preserves what the source is doing—not whether it mirrors its form.

Established translation procedures make room for that distinction. Transposition changes grammatical form; modulation changes the conceptual viewpoint or phrasing; equivalence uses a different target-language expression for a similar communicative effect; and adaptation adjusts a reference or formulation to the target context. These are ways to depart from formal correspondence while seeking to preserve meaning [8], [9]. Nida’s account likewise emphasizes interpretation within cultural context, rather than word-for-word substitution [10].

A practical sequence follows from this literature:

  1. Establish the source meaning, referents, pragmatic force, and intended audience before choosing target wording.
  2. Render that meaning using target-language grammar and conventional phrasing, even when this requires a different word class, image, or sentence structure.
  3. For idioms, metaphors, and culture-bound expressions, check whether the target form preserves the intended function; a literal version may be appropriate only when the image itself matters and remains intelligible.
  4. Use sentence and document context to resolve ambiguity, maintain terminology, and preserve voice and discourse links. In literary translation experiments, paragraph-level context improved human preferences over sentence-isolated LLM translation, but errors and omissions remained [11].
  5. Review the final text against both the source and target-language norms. A target-only fluency check can miss meaning errors; a source-only literalness check can penalize valid, natural reformulations.

Evidence on whether AI systems are less literal is mixed, in part because studies test different models, language pairs, genres, and definitions of literality. One study found GPT-3-era outputs tended to be less literal than NMT in several English-to-other-language settings, especially for idioms, but explicitly cautioned that being less literal does not prove that an idiom’s meaning was translated correctly [12]. A literary-translation study found human translations less syntactically similar to their sources and more varied than LLM outputs, which it interpreted as evidence that the tested machines remained more literal [13]. A 2026 study using a heuristic literality index found that several tested LLMs became less literal through iterative revision, but its data were English-source and the index is not a complete measure of translation quality [14]. The evidence therefore supports checking for calques and source-shaped phrasing, not assuming that any system or style of prompting will reliably avoid them.

What translation quality ratings are available?

The word “rating” can refer to several different things: a human score, a categorized error record, an automatic metric, or a measure of the work required to make a translation usable. They answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Adequacy or accuracy rating

What it recordsHow well the translation conveys source meaning. Often paired with a separate fluency rating [15].

Useful forComparing meaning transfer, especially when raters can see the source.

Main cautionA single adequacy score can hide which meaning errors occurred and how serious they are.

Fluency or naturalness rating

What it recordsHow idiomatic and well-formed the target text seems.

Useful forAssessing readability and target-language expression.

Main cautionWithout the source, it cannot establish faithfulness. Ratings can also depend on genre and audience.

Scalar quality rating or direct assessment

What it recordsA holistic human score or judgment, such as a numeric rating or “better/worse” comparison [15], [16].

Useful forEfficient comparison of systems or outputs at scale.

Main cautionScores depend on instructions, raters, context, and calibration; a number is not a universal quality scale.

Pairwise ranking or best–worst scaling

What it recordsWhich of several translations is preferred, or which are best and worst.

Useful forComparing strong alternatives when absolute scoring is hard.

Main cautionGives less diagnostic information about what to fix; literary evaluations show that evaluator expertise and task design can shift results [13].

MQM error annotation

What it recordsError spans categorized under a configurable taxonomy, such as accuracy, fluency, terminology, or style; severity can also be recorded [1].

Useful forDiagnosing errors and prioritizing revisions. Professional, context-aware MQM evaluation produced different system rankings from crowd judgments in a large study [16].

Main cautionMore labor-intensive; categories and weights must fit the task. MQM is a framework, not one universal score.

Post-editing effort

What it recordsTime, edits, keystrokes, pauses, or other process measures needed to make output usable [5].

Useful forEstimating workflow cost and identifying difficult text or error types.

Main cautionEffort is not the same as final quality; edit counts, time, and cognitive effort are not interchangeable.

Reference-based automatic metrics

What it recordsSimilarity to one or more human references. BLEU uses word n-gram overlap; ChrF uses character n-grams; learned metrics such as COMET use model representations trained or assessed against human judgments [17], [18], [19].

Useful forFast, repeatable system comparison and development monitoring.

Main cautionValid paraphrases may differ from references; scores do not directly verify meaning, naturalness, or task suitability.

Reference-free quality estimation

What it recordsPredicts likely quality without a reference translation; may help route outputs for review [20].

Useful forTriage when a reference is unavailable.

Main cautionA confident score can still reflect bias. A 2026 idiom benchmark found many tested metrics and LLM judges preferred literal over idiomatic translations.

BLEU is a clear example of both the value and limits of automatic scoring. It compares word-sequence overlap with references and was proposed as a quick, repeatable aid to human evaluation—not a direct semantic test. A translation can preserve meaning with different wording and still receive a lower overlap score [17]. Newer learned metrics often align more closely with human judgments in benchmark comparisons, but performance varies across languages and tasks; no metric in a major comparison matched human judgments perfectly [18]. COMET, for example, predicts human-derived quality signals and performs strongly on several benchmarks, but its score remains a model estimate, not a human review or guarantee of correctness [19].

MQM is useful when the question is not just “Which output scored higher?” but “What went wrong, and how serious is it?” It lets a project define an error taxonomy and severity scheme for its needs rather than imposing one set of weights on every text. The distinction matters: a style issue may be central in marketing copy or literary work and secondary in a routine service notice; a terminology error may be critical in technical or legal material [1]. For literary translation in particular, complex error annotation by inexperienced evaluators can misjudge deliberate choices; professional judgment or simpler comparative tasks may be more suitable [13].

Current issues for AI translation and evaluation

One score can conceal a tradeoff. Accuracy and naturalness are often combined into a single quality number, though they are distinct aims. A 2025 study formalizes an accuracy–naturalness frontier and recommends reporting both dimensions; the precise tradeoff depends on its metric definitions and reference distribution, so its framework should not be read as a universal law for every translation task [2]. For practical evaluation, separate scores are more informative than a single blended rank.

Idioms expose metric bias. A 2026 benchmark found that many tested automatic quality-estimation systems, including LLM judges, scored literal idiom translations above idiomatic ones. Only a small subset of the tested systems consistently preferred idiomatic translations across its nine target languages. The benchmark is limited to English-source idioms and curated examples, but it shows why higher automated scores should not be assumed to reward the intended meaning [20].

Fluency can mask serious errors. Hallucination research on multilingual translation models found substantially higher detected rates in low-resource directions than in high-resource ones, with detached or source-unrelated outputs among the failure types. Rates depend on the models and detection method in that study, but the broader warning is important: smooth output is not proof of fidelity, and language-resource levels affect risk [21].

Context changes what can be judged. Sentence-level assessment can miss inconsistent terms, pronoun resolution, cohesion, or discourse meaning. In one Chinese-to-English news study, professional raters who saw whole documents preferred human translations over MT on adequacy, where sentence-level judgments had not shown a significant preference [22]. Contextual prompting helps some LLM translation tasks, but does not remove omissions or critical mistakes [11].

Quality depends on purpose and stakes. Gisting, public communication, literary translation, and medical or legal use have different error tolerances. A translation fit for getting the gist may not be fit for a consent form or legal proceeding. Reviews of medical and legal MT describe both access benefits and risks from consequential errors, confidentiality, and uneven quality across languages; they support deployment rules and review based on the actual use, not a generic benchmark score [23]. Human-centered MT research similarly argues for evaluating systems within real tasks and retaining meaningful human control [6].

A practical quality-rating set

For a service or research comparison intended to test whether translations convey meaning naturally rather than calque source wording, a compact evaluation should report at least:

  • Meaning preservation: expert, source-visible adequacy judgments plus MQM-tagged major meaning errors such as mistranslation or omission.
  • Naturalness and idiomaticity: a separate target-language judgment, with explicit attention to idioms, collocations, register, and source-shaped syntax.
  • Context and consistency: document-level review of referents, terminology, discourse links, and voice.
  • Task fit: whether the text serves its intended audience and use, including risk-sensitive review for high-stakes content.
  • Workflow cost: post-editing time or effort when comparing service models, kept separate from output quality.
  • Automatic scores: BLEU, ChrF, COMET, or quality-estimation scores as supporting evidence, not as a substitute for the dimensions above.

This combination makes disagreements informative. A system that is accurate but stiff differs from one that reads naturally but omits source details; a single score can hide the distinction. The right balance depends on the intended translation service, but any claim of quality should make clear whose judgment was used, what the rating measured, and whether raters saw the source and surrounding context [1], [2], [16].
